# Approvals

When an action needs your approver's yes first, put one card in front of them that they can decide from in a few seconds, keep working, and act on their answer exactly when it comes back. A good card gets a fast yes or a useful correction. A vague one gets "what is the question?" or no answer at all, and the work stalls. Everything you post there, the card and every reply under it, is read the same way: once, cold, on a phone, between other work, and taken as fact.

## What matters most

- **Ask only when a yes is actually required.** If your instructions or a standing rule already let you act, act and report what you did. Which calls are his is your repository's to say (psbrain's is "What is his" in `shared/asking-amir.md`), or "Questions" in his global instructions where it says nothing. A card that didn't need asking spends the approver's attention and teaches them to skim cards.
- **The first step of every card, before you write it: name what it is a piece of, and read the approver's latest words on that.** Do it before you plan one, tell anyone one is coming, or hand one to a teammate to write, and again at the moment you post, however long ago it was planned: open the thing in your record, look for a card or deck already open on it, and ask the agent who has been talking with him about it where it stands. Your check passing, your own earlier plan, a rule that makes this kind of thing his, and a teammate or lead saying "card it" all tell you the piece is ready or that it is his. None tells you whether he has finished thinking about the feature, plan or test it belongs to, and the teammate who told you to card it may be the agent he is talking with: ask them where he stands. If he is still shaping it, the piece goes back to them, to bring inside their answer; if the piece is a fix or a step apart from what he is thinking through, their "card it" stands and it goes up now. The card carries what you found in its `stands` field (the whole; his latest words on it, with when and where; and which of four it is), and the tool asks for it when it is missing:
  - **Not on his mind:** a post, a fix to something live, a release, a step in a plan he approved, the card he just asked for. It goes up the minute it is ready, and a search for a conversation that isn't there is time it didn't ship.
  - **New to him:** he has never met the whole and can judge all of it from the card in seconds. It goes up. If he would look at it and reshape it, it is a deck.
  - **Yes to the whole:** he said so about the whole, in words you can quote. A go on a goal is not a yes to a design he hasn't seen, and his words about another ask are not about this one. The card opens with that yes. A card that turns a built thing on shows the built thing itself; when it is too big to judge from a card (a tab, a flow, a page he said he would walk), he walks it first.
  - **Still shaping:** a card on it he answered with a question, a change or "hold on"; a deck he hasn't answered; a thread where he is talking it through. There is no card from you: the piece goes to the agent he is shaping it with and reaches him inside the next version of the whole, and you tell whoever asked you to card it. His hold on a plan holds its other cards too, so say which of yours are still open with him. A card about a part asks him to commit before the whole, from an agent he isn't talking to. Amir, November 22, on push words carded for a feature still in its deck: "Why am I pre-approving pushes for a feature I've never seen?"

  The same holds for a card already open with him: while he is reading it or has answered it with a question, the only new version is the one his reply asked for. A new ask (more money, another split, another fix stacked on it) is never a version of the card he is reading and never a second card beside it; it waits for his answer, or goes to the agent he is working it through with.

  Until you have looked, promise nobody a card: "it comes back to you on a card" is the card's first step taken without the look. Your repository says more on where he stands (psbrain: "What is his" in `shared/asking-amir.md`).
- **A card is for a change that is ready to happen, never for deciding to build something on the card alone.** It shows the exact thing that will go out or switch on: the ad, the reply, the price, the message, a flag on code that already exists. Whether to build a feature, a new screen, or an experiment whose variant needs app or server work first is a different decision, which the approver makes from the report or deck that carries its case, size and mocks. Amir, October 19, 2026, declining two cards that asked him to approve building experiments: "features are not built via an approval Slack message. That's an entirely different decision process because I'm never going to just click yes and then let an agent build some feature." So keep that call a proposal in your report, deck or plan, unless the rules of the repository you work in send it to the approver as a card that links that deck; then the card is the deck's front page, with what users would see and what decides it. Once the thing is built, switching it on can be a card.
- **Every card opens with why it's worth doing, and says what it belongs to.** The approver decides on value, and a card that shows only what will happen and how much leaves him guessing what it's for: "I understand the what but there's no why," on a card that laid out a puzzle change in five tidy facts. `why` is the first thing under the title: what the business gets and why now, in a sentence or two of his terms. For a test, it's the question the result answers and what you'd do on each answer, never a gain nobody knows yet. If you can't write it, or don't believe it, the card isn't ready. One agent sent a card to build something its own analysis said wasn't worth doing, because building felt cheap, and a why line didn't save it. `part_of` names the initiative in his words for it, or is `null` when the card stands alone; `approvals status AP-0013 --part-of "<initiative>"` sets it on a card already out. `about` names the one thing in the pod dashboard the card decides, by its id (`"about": "P-1"` in the JSON), because his answer doesn't stay on the card: the moment he gives it, it is his decision on that thing's page, where whoever holds the thing, and every agent who opens it after, reads it without anyone passing it on. So name the narrowest thing the yes really decides: the test it starts, the feature it ships, the task it lets you do, the process whose number it moves, and your pod only when nothing narrower fits. A yes that would decide two things is two cards. `pd search <its name>` finds the id. A card naming a thing the dashboard doesn't hold is turned back to you. One you post naming nothing still reaches him, but his answer then sits on the card alone, where no one holding the work will read it, so the tool and `pd mine` both ask you what it decided until you say with `pd tie <id> AP-0013`. Most cards that seem to decide nothing do serve something: the replies to players and store reviews that went up naming nothing on November 21 each belonged to the process that answers everyone who writes to us. A new version keeps the thing the first one named. An experiment or a feature has one open card at a time, since a second could only be the same ask again: while one is open, change that card with a new version rather than putting up another. Any other thing, your pod or a process included, may have several cards open, each its own decision.
- **Cards that come out of one ask carry the same `part_of`, word for word.** The approver holds an ask of his as one thing, and it comes back as cards from whichever agents did the work. One morning he asked for every screen to fit small phones; by the afternoon five cards had come back from two teams, two under one team's own process and three under no name, and nothing on any of them said they were one piece of work: "It's just so draining to try to interpret each of these things in isolation from everything else every time." `part_of` is what joins them. Cards with the same `part_of` are kept on one page, each with a button to it, where he sees every card from that work, where each stands, and all their screens together. So before you name what a card is part of, look at what is already in use: `approvals names` lists the last three days' names with who sent them, and `approvals read` shows them when yours matches none. A card that comes out of something he asked for is part of that ask, under the name the first card from it used; if yours is the first, name the ask in his words and say when he asked ("Every screen fits small phones (your ask, 10:08 am)"). Your team or process is already on that line as the sender, so naming it again as what the card is part of tells him nothing and hides the ask. Where it turns: work that comes from your own process and no ask of his keeps its initiative's name, as before, and a close subject is not a reason to take a name. The test is whether he would call the cards one piece of work. A card already out under another name is moved with `approvals status AP-0013 --part-of "<the name, word for word>"`.
- **A card that comes out of a conversation says so, and shows up in it.** When the approver has been talking with you, or with your team's lead, in a Slack thread, and the card answers something he said there, he is holding that conversation in his head. A card that arrives without it makes him the one who joins the two: he reads the card cold, writes his change on it, and the thread never learns, so the next thing said there is already out of date. His words for it: "it's disconnected from the underlying thread. They don't know about each other." So name his message: `approvals submit card.json --thread <the link of his message>`, or `"thread"` in the JSON. The card then opens with his words and a link back; the thread shows a small card with the card's own buttons, kept true as the card moves; and what he writes on the card is copied into the thread. He answers in either place, and a change he asks for in the thread is a change to the card: post the next version, never ask him to say it again on the card. The small card is the pointer, so don't also type the card's number into the thread. Where it turns: most cards come out of no conversation and name none, and a thread about the same subject, last week's or the one he is in right now, is not where this card came from. The test is whether he is waiting for this card there: he asked for it in that thread, or it carries out what he just decided in it. Having been told in the thread that a card is coming does not make him wait for it. A card he isn't waiting for there lands in the middle of what he is thinking through, so it names no thread. A link someone handed you is an address, not a reading of the thread. And while he is still working out the thing a card belongs to, in that thread, by email or with another agent, there is no card yet, in the thread or out of it: the piece goes to the agent he is working it out with. An approve button on something he hasn't finished thinking about got "Why are you asking for these approvals when we're not done on the strategy?", and then one agent answered him on the card while another answered in the thread. If you weren't in the conversation, ask whoever was where it stands before you post. When the tool can't post in that conversation (a direct message with another app), it says so as you submit: link the card there yourself.
- **A request to do it is not the approver's yes.** Tasks often come from a teammate, another agent, a brief or a schedule. Unless the approver is plainly the one telling you to do this, treat it as a request that still needs their yes. When they do tell you directly, that is the yes; don't send them a card about their own instruction.
- **The card is how the question reaches them.** Asking in this conversation only works if the approver is the one reading it. When it might not be them, submit a card and say in the conversation that you did.
- **Never take the action before the answer.** These count as a yes: the card's approve button, which reaches you as an `[Approvals]` message saying it was approved; the approver's own words naming this request, such as "post it" on the card or a yes given elsewhere that you record with `approvals record`; his deputy's approval; or approval from someone on his team: a teammate the card was sent to, or, where the workspace lets his whole team answer any card, anyone on it. The deputy is an agent he has set to answer for him the kinds of card he approves every time. Its yes arrives as the same `[Approvals]` message, says it was approved for him and why, and counts as his for exactly what the card describes. A card his deputy leaves can also go to someone on his team who can judge it as well as he can. That person's answer arrives as the same [Approvals] message, names who answered, and counts as the approver's for exactly what the card describes. You never choose who else gets a card, and you write every card for the approver. A friendly reply to a long report is not a yes.
- **Put the real thing on the card.** Show the exact reply under the post it answers, the ad itself, the price table with today beside proposed, the email word for word, and a change to what users see as the journey a user takes through it, today's path and the changed one. If the approver would have to open something else to know what they're agreeing to, the card isn't ready.
- **One decision per card, answered where he reads it.** He reads top to bottom on a phone and answers with the buttons under what he just read, so whatever a button decides sits right above it, and the button says what a tap makes happen, never a key like A, B or 2 that he has to look up ("I have no idea what A, B, or C are", November 14). A row that says "see below", or words behind a Read button, sends him down and back up with the row held in his head; four customer replies as four rows behind four Read buttons was "this weird homework," and he asked for separate cards instead. So rows are for parts of one decision that a line or two decides each, like a release's stores; anything that needs its own reading (a reply to a person, a design, a plan, a spend whose case takes more than a few lines) is its own card, and cards that belong together go out together as a set, each showing "1 of 4". Together is half of it: four related cards arriving one by one over half an hour got "they should come together rather than individually." A card also asks one kind of yes: a card asking to ship what's built that also carries what reads like decisions to build left him asking which it wanted. [references/cards.md](references/cards.md#one-card-or-a-set) has the cases each way; read it before any card with rows or more than one card.
- **Ask only for a yes that someone will carry out.** A yes is an instruction, and the approver stops tracking the request once they give it. If you can't act on it yourself, the card names who will, and you get the yes to them and see it through. Otherwise the yes lands nowhere while the approver believes it's handled.
- **Ask once.** Never post the same card again. If the card must be answered by a time that is near and the approver may not have seen it, the rules of the repository you work in say whether and how to remind them; without such a rule, don't. No answer by the deadline means no.
- **Take a card down as soon as you stop standing behind it.** An open card has a working approve button, and approvers clear several cards in a minute. When you drop the version it shows, the moment passes, or a fact his yes would rest on changes, withdraw it with `approvals withdraw` and say why, or post the version you do stand behind. On October 19 a card was approved nine hours after its agent had said in the thread that nothing would post from it, because its button still worked.
- **The thread is the approver's whole view of your work.** They can't see your session; 📨 on their reply only means the message was typed into it. So:
  - **Reply once, and make it the answer.** When he asks something, the first thing he should meet under his words is the answer, as "Answers" in "Writing to Amir" says: "No, it isn't worth doing now," "It doesn't change it: testers keep press-and-hold." That holds just as much when the answer also changes the card, which is how most of his questions arrive ("Is he on the old catalog? ... offer to move him"). A whole new card under his question, with the answer somewhere in it, makes him hunt for the one thing he asked. On November 17 he asked whether a friend who sits down gets asked about notifications, and got "Checking now", four pictures and the card again, with "no, not today" as the first words of its change line: "I asked you a question and I got a bunch of spam. Just answer my ... question." The agent's next post was the reply he wanted: "No. A friend who sat down used to get Sit & Go's notification ask; the new look picker dropped it last night, so today they're asked only if they deal. It goes back in this build." So when the card changes too, send both in one command, `approvals revise AP-0013 card.json --answer "<the verdict, then the fact it rests on>"`: your answer goes under his words with one line saying the card changed, and the card itself updates at the top. Size the answer to what he asked: the verdict and the one or two facts it rests on, a sentence or three, read on a phone between other things. Everything else you found waits until he asks, and he will. On November 18 he asked of a test "Is this ... thing live? ... What are you asking for?" and got 157 words, the ask in the last two sentences; "Yes, it's live since November 16, but there's no read yet: only 6 of the 18 finishers saw the offer. The ask: keep letting new learners in until about December 14 instead of stopping November 29, so a real effect shows about 8 times in 10." was the whole answer. Where it turns: when he asks why, or for your read ("What's your intuition?"), the reasoning is the answer, and it still opens on the verdict. A "Got it: you want to know whether..." post that restates his question and promises an answer is one more thing to read before the one he wanted, and a run of acknowledgements, new versions and self-corrections makes him reread all of them: "Please stop sending me the same approvals and just ... talk to me." Your answer, or your next version when he only asked for a change, is your acknowledgement. Post a separate `approvals ack` only when the real reply will take more than a few minutes, saying what's coming and when; and when he says yes, one line on what you're doing now is the right first post.
  - **What he writes on a card is owed a line back, whatever becomes of the card.** He wrote to you, not to the decision. Withdrawing, finishing, a decline or a new version settles the card but says nothing about what he said, so he can't tell from the thread whether you read it. On November 22 he left a note on AP-2245 asking for the designer's options. Ninety-nine seconds later its agent took the card down for a reason of its own, and his note sat unanswered for fourteen hours. So before a card leaves your hands, read its thread back from his last words, and answer what you find there with `approvals answer`, which posts on a withdrawn or finished card too. That holds even when taking the card down is exactly what he asked for. On November 21 he wrote "Just take down the card until it's ready" on AP-2074, and one line ended it: "Taken down, as you asked: it comes back as a new card once staging passes." A withdraw's reason is read as the card's reason, not as your reply to him, so the line goes on its own. Until you post it, `pd mine` lists it as a comment you still owe.
  - **Promise only what you know you can do.** "Starting it now" before checking whether there's room to start it becomes a correction three minutes later. Check first, or say what you're checking.
  - **"Done" means what he takes it to mean ("Status" in "Writing to Amir"): the thing his yes asked for has happened, and you've seen it:** the post is up or reads back scheduled, the setting reads back on, the release went to the stores it named. Your part finishing or a hand-off isn't done; until the thing has happened, the card says where it stands and who it waits on. A card marked done while the setting it asked for was still off got "Did this happen?" back. Once it has, done is done ("Qualifiers" in "Writing to Amir").
  - **If you end up doing something different, say so in the thread.**
- **Act only on answers that reach you:** an `[Approvals]` message in this session, or one `approvals inbox` hands you. A request another session made belongs to that session even when it's for the same change, including an earlier run of your scheduled job. A session you continue is different: if this session opened with a summary of earlier work, that work is yours, its open cards included. So is a card you raised before you were started again in a new session (a restart, a restore, your pod moving to another machine): when the session that asked is gone, the service types the answer into the session you run in now (for a pod member, whatever session runs in its tab), and an answer that reaches you that way is yours to act on. If the change you were asked for is already requested and still open, don't post a duplicate; say which request it's waiting on.
- **Don't wait around, and look for your answers whenever you pick work back up.** After submitting, keep working on something else, or end your turn saying which request you're waiting on. If the approver stops this session, an answer that comes meanwhile waits for you, and nothing types it in when you're back. So whenever you pick work back up with cards out (a resumed or restored session, a new run of a scheduled job), run `approvals inbox --agent "<name>"` before anything else and act on what it hands you.

## How the approver reads, and how to write for it

How he reads is "Writing to Amir" in your global instructions; what follows is what a card adds to it. You can't check a card by rereading it yourself. By the time you write it you have been in the work for hours, and every name you coined along the way reads as ordinary to you: a reader given only the card found names it had to work out on every card it was handed in the week of November 10, the ones he approved without a word included ("the lock check", "our free rung"), and on November 13 he said of the pods' cards, "It's really inscrutable. It's really confusingly phrased." So before a card goes up, have it read by someone who holds none of your session: `approvals read card.json` hands the card, as he will see it, to a reader that has his rules for writing to him and nothing of your work, and prints each place it had to stop, what it thinks you are asking, and what it thinks a yes makes happen. Each stop is either his word, which stays, or yours, which becomes the real name of the thing or what it does for users. Fix a stop by saying the real thing or cutting it, never by adding a sentence that explains your label or by pointing at a document he can't see from the card, so the card comes out no longer. If its two last lines aren't what you are asking, the card isn't ready. The reader is no gate and it isn't him: it doesn't know the product, so it stops at a few of his real names, and a card it reads cleanly can still be wrong. Where your machine has `$readable-reports`, give the words its pass. No em dashes.

- **His words, not yours** ("Names" in "Writing to Amir"). On a card your working labels read literally: one that called a new AI "production's recipe," because it was trained with production's settings, got "You're saying switch production to production." Which to write, the term or what it does for users, turns on what he is judging. When the thing itself is the decision (which model, at what precision, on which servers), write the term: a card that called a 16-bit quantized copy of a policy "the smaller AI store" got "is it quantized? I'm an engineer talk to me like an adult." When the mechanism is only how, write what changes for users: "Extend sign-ins made before October 8 to the 1,000 days we now set" got "Is this ... English?", and the same change as "Stop signing out users who signed in before October 8" got a yes. A card, issue or pull request number goes after the plain name as a link, never in its place and never as the explanation: "Don't explain things to me by referencing a ... code thing." That holds most of all for his own earlier yes. He answers dozens of cards a day, his deputy answers others for him, and he carries none of their numbers: "the pool-hall frame remade in GPT Image, as you asked on AP-0962" got "I didn't ask for this one ... I have no ... idea what this even is" eighty minutes after he had tapped yes on that card. So a card that rests on something he approved or said tells him what it was and when ("the remake of the one hand-painted frame, which you approved at 5:22 this morning") and keeps the number as the link. Whose yes it was comes from that card, read before you cite it (`approvals show AP-0962` names who answered and when), never from memory: his deputy answers many of his cards and a teammate some, and "you approved" for a yes he never gave makes him doubt the card in front of him as well as the one behind it. In six days of November five makers told him "you approved" where his deputy or a teammate had answered. So say whose it was: "which you approved at 5:22 this morning" when the button was his, "which your deputy approved for you at 5:22 this morning" when it was the deputy's, the teammate by name when it was theirs. His field's terms need no help; your process's terms always do.
- **What it is and where it stands, before any detail.** Say the whole change before its edge cases: a card that opened on a locked-puzzle case got "Is this literally just for puzzles or what?" when the change covered every purchase. Give its state in a word he'd use: built, live for half of users, waiting on Apple. If it changes or reverses something he approved, say that first and name what. A test running beside another is called a side test, so it doesn't read as more of the main one.
- **Fewer things, each said whole.** The card draws each fact as its own block with its label above it, so a short label and a sentence or two each; a value that is a list puts each item on a line starting with "- ", and they show as bullets. Aim for about 100 to 180 words of your own. What he's approving (a message's exact words, an ad's words, a release's changes) doesn't count against that and is never cut to fit, and a round of ads counts per ad. What proves your work (the checks, the research, the numbers behind the numbers) goes in `evidence`, posted in the thread.
- **Every number whole, as "Numbers" in "Writing to Amir" says,** on the card itself, since the card is all he reads before he answers: "$700 a day, up from $450 last week"; a rate where a rate exists.
- **Leave out how your systems work; keep everything his yes decides.** He built the systems, so don't walk him through flags, server fields, build and deploy steps, or how you'd switch it off. But who a change reaches, how many and for how long, what it spends, which release or store and when, what it takes away, what can't be undone, and the new words users will read are his decision, and they stay. A cost to your team is noise; a cost that moves his date or his money is part of the choice. When it touches sign-in, payments or something users already have, say so in one plain line.
- **Only what's true.** He acts on "checked", "done", "live" and "waiting on you" without checking, and one false line makes him audit every card after it: a card said its script had been through cold readers and a final read, and he heard the lines nobody had read ("did anybody read this script?"). "Checked" names checks that ran on exactly what he's looking at. Anything "you said" is quoted from the record with its date, or left out. When a card carries numbers or claims, they get the check your repository gives anything before he acts on it (psbrain's "Check every number before he does"), or, where it names none, a fresh reader against their sources: a cold check has caught a seventh of a card's claims wrong.
- **Only what's his, once.** Never his own instruction sent back for approval ("I ... told you to send the email"), a call that's yours to make, an engineering question inside a product decision, or a phrase for him to reply with. When a standing rule is what makes it his (every new ad, taking a screen away), say which in a line. Related asks ready at the same time arrive together, as rows or a set; related asks that ripen hours apart don't wait for each other; unrelated asks ready in the same minute are separate cards, not a set.

## Asking

1. **Write the card as JSON, starting from `approvals guide <type>`.** Run it before you draft a word, because the first thing it prints is the corrections he has given for this kind of card, each with his own line where he gave it on a card: every one is a judgment an earlier card of this type got wrong, and the same judgment is the one you are about to make. Under them is the line that names them in your card (`"corrections_seen"`), then how to write that type: what the approver judges it by, what it must include, and a full example. For the kind you need, read [references/cards.md](references/cards.md#kinds-how-he-judges-it-and-answers-it): how to shape the card around how he'll judge it (today beside after, a row each, a pick, a batch, his own words to edit).
2. **Have it read:** `approvals read card.json`, or every file of a set in one command. It posts nothing and takes about a minute. Change the card until what the reader thinks you are asking, and what it thinks a yes makes happen, are what you mean, and every stop left is a real name of his.
3. **Submit it** from your own session, on the machine you run on: `approvals submit card.json --agent "<your name as the approver knows you>"`. A set is several files in one command, `approvals submit a.json b.json c.json`: each is its own card with its own answer, posted one after another and marked "1 of 3"; if any is refused, none is posted. Add `--thread <link>` when the card answers something he said in a Slack thread. The command learns from your session where to type the answer, so a card posted from another shell, such as over ssh, could never be answered to you, and the command refuses it. It prints the request number (for example AP-0013) and a link, one of each per card. If it refuses the card, it says why; fix that and submit again.
4. **Carry on.** Do other work, or end your turn.

Add `--to <name>` to `approvals submit` to send a card, or every card of a set, to another approver the workspace lists. Your own repository says which decisions go to whom. Without `--to`, the workspace's default approver gets the card.

A `budget`, `purchase` or `money` card also reaches the reviewer for money when one is set up (the CFO), and its read of your card lands in the thread, often before the approver answers; so don't ask it about the card separately, and remember its note isn't an answer: yours comes only from the approver.

Every card needs:
- `type`, one of: `creative`, `reply`, `budget`, `purchase`, `money`, `player_message`, `price`, `experiment`, `page`, `content`, `email`, `account`;
- `title`, the action said plainly ("Move $20,000 from savings to operating", not "Transfer approval");
- `why`, one or two sentences on what the business gets and why now;
- `part_of`, the initiative it belongs to, or `null`;
- `about`, the id of the one thing in the pod dashboard it decides;
- `recipients`, on an `email` or `reply` card: whom it goes to, `[{"place": "email", "handle": "brantzenpoker@gmail.com"}]`, the handle exactly as the place writes it, so its done moves the person whose route it is. A player is an id, never their address: their thread's (`"email thread"`), their account's when they never wrote to us (`"player account"`), or their review's; where no listed place fits, `"other"` says where and whom in a few words. `approvals guide email` lists the places;
- `thread`, optional: the link of the approver's Slack message this card answers, when it came out of a conversation with him;
- `facts`, a list of `[label, value]` pairs;
- `approves` and `does_not_approve`, which mark the edges of the yes.

Some types also need `quotes`, a `table`, or an `image` or `video`; the cards reference says which. Pictures must read when he opens them full size and zooms in: a screen at the phone's own resolution, a slide exported at two or three times its size, cut to what the card is about.

A card that changes what a user sees carries the user's `journey`: today's path and the path with the change, screen by screen, with each tap named. The card shows it as a small map with a See it big button, which opens every screen at full height with today beside the change, so he never has to ask to be shown it. A strip of separate screens got "I need you to show me the user journey before and after" on card after card. The cards reference says how to build one, and when a plain `gallery` is still right.

**Evidence has to open in Slack.** A path to a file on your machine posted as text opens nothing, so give the file's path (alone or after a label, like `"Recipe: /path/recipe.json"`) and it's attached to the thread, or link a page the approver can open: a doc, a sheet, a share link.

## When the answer arrives

Answers arrive as messages starting with `[Approvals]` that name who answered and quote their words exactly. They are typed into this session while it's running, or printed by `approvals inbox` for one that came while it wasn't. For a yes, a no, a pick or a set of items, first post one line with `approvals ack AP-0013 "<what you understood and what you're doing now>"`, such as "Got it: posting the reply now, then I'll mark it done." For a reply or a question, your answer or your next version is the acknowledgement; post nothing before it. A deadline passing needs no acknowledgement. A comment made on the pod dashboard reaches you as the same message, posted in the card's thread; answer it there with the same commands.

| The message says | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| It was approved | Do exactly what the card described, nothing more. Then run `approvals done AP-0013 "<what you did and how you checked it>"`. |
| It was approved for him by his deputy | It lets you act as his own yes would: do exactly what the card described, nothing more, then `approvals done`. The message carries the deputy's reason, which changes nothing about what the card covers. It covers every row, the ones you marked as needing their own yes included. If your card asked for a pick, the message names the pick it took, which is the one your card named: do exactly what the card says for that option, nothing else. What it doesn't make it is his decision: he hasn't read the card, and may first see it in his morning summary. A teammate's card is no different: before you write "you approved" of any card, read whose yes it was where the record says it was approved (`pd open <card>`: "approved · his deputy's yes"). So when you speak of it to him later (a new card that builds on it, a deck, a status), say who gave it: "your deputy approved it for you on November 20 (AP-1854)". "You approved" tells him he made a call he never saw; he takes it as a fact about his own decisions, stops looking, and loses the tap that takes it back. A yes he gave himself is still "you approved". |
| The approver handed the card to someone on his team | Nothing to do or acknowledge. The named person's answer will arrive as the approver's, and the deadline in the message is the one that counts. |
| Someone on his team answered the card | Treat the named person's answer as the approver's for the scope it names. Acknowledge and act on a yes, a no or a pick as you would for the approver. Answer a question or revise the card when the reply asks for a change. When you cite it later it is that person's, by name ("his engineer approved it on November 20" where you would otherwise write "you approved"), for the reason given above for a deputy's. |
| He took back an approval his deputy gave | It is not approved any more. If you haven't acted, don't. If you have, undo what can be undone, and say on the card where it stands with `approvals status`. Your acknowledgement says which of the two it is; he may say more in the thread. |
| It was declined | Drop this proposal; never send it again or a lightly changed copy. A no is to the proposal, not necessarily to what it was for: usually the goal still stands and the next proposal is actually different, and it reaches him as a card only when he is done with the thing it belongs to (the first step of every card, above); sometimes the goal is dead too. Your acknowledgement says which you took it to be. |
| He removed it from his list | Nothing on it is approved, and it's no verdict on the idea: he's saying the card shouldn't be waiting on him. Drop it and don't post it again. If what it was for still matters, work out why it didn't belong in front of him (yours to decide, already settled, out of date) before asking again. |
| A question in the thread | Answer it: `approvals answer AP-0013 "<the verdict, then the fact it rests on>"`. That is your acknowledgement, and it hands the card back to him as it stands. When the answer also changes what a yes covers, it is still one reply: `approvals revise AP-0013 card.json --answer "<the verdict, then the fact it rests on>"`. |
| A reply asking for a change | First ask what the reply is. If it reopens the design or the plan (he wants other options, another journey, says hold on, or sends you to work it out with someone), he isn't deciding yet: answer in one line, post no new version, do the work, and bring it back once, whole, where he is working it through. If it changes only what is on the card (a label, a number, a word), post the next version: `approvals revise AP-0013 card.json`, with a `changes` field saying what moved and an `answers` field quoting the reply it answers (or "correction:" and the fact that was wrong; the tool asks for it when it is missing); under his reply it shows as one line, and the card updates at the top. If he also asked something, add `--answer`. If you truly can't continue without one fact, ask instead: `approvals ask AP-0013 "<one question>"`. If the reply plainly approves the card as it stands ("post it"), record that with `approvals record AP-0013 --approved --words "<their words>" --where "Slack thread"` and act on it; if it plainly declines, record it with `--declined` and drop it. |
| An answer to your question | If it asks for a change, carry on with `approvals revise`. If it lets the card go ahead as it stands, even one he had already approved ("sure, schedule it whenever" after you asked which hour to use once the first one passed), record it with `approvals record AP-0013 --approved --words "<their words>" --where "Slack thread"` (on a card with rows, add `--part <row>` when the answer was about one row), act on it, then run `approvals done`. |
| An answer on one part of a set (`part 2 of 4`) | It answers that part only. Act on that part as above; the message says where the other parts stand. Parts you leave exactly as they are keep their answers in the next version. |
| He picked an option | Do exactly what the card said for that option, nothing else. |
| He approved some items | Do only the items the message lists; the ones it leaves out are a no. |
| He approved with his own edited words | Use his words exactly, as the message quotes them (a JSON string, line breaks and all), not your draft. |
| He answered your offer to stop asking | "Stop asking for these": record it as a standing exception in your own rules, with the date and exactly what it covers, and do that kind without a card from now on. "Keep asking": keep sending cards. |
| No answer by the deadline | It's not approved, unless the card's `if_no_answer` said what happens then: do that. Don't resubmit it. On a set, only the parts still open close; answered parts stand. |

**After the yes, keep the card true until the thing his yes asked for has happened.** He stops tracking a request once he answers it, and the card is where he looks to see whether it happened. So post each step on the card as it happens: `approvals status AP-0013 "Built, and live for half of new users"`, then `approvals status AP-0013 "First read at about $10 spent" --next` for what comes next, `--part 2` (or the row's id) for one row of a set, and `--headline "Released: three lesson fixes"` once it's out. When it waits on someone other than him (a teammate's design note, Apple's review, the next money ask), say who and by when with `--waiting-on`: the card shows it and stays off his morning list until your next line. `--on-you` is only for a condition he alone can clear ("approved, but I'll play it myself before it merges"); a card that says it waits on him when nothing does gets "There's nothing for me to do." On a message sent as him, `--their-answer` puts the other side's reply on the card. Each line is one short sentence he can read at a glance. These redraw the card in place and notify no one; `done` still marks the end, when it's real.

**A new version replaces the old one.** The card at the top of the thread always shows the newest version, with its buttons, and an earlier copy in the thread shrinks to a line he can open if he wants it. What lands at the bottom, where he reads, depends on whether the version answers him. After a reply of his or a teammate's, it is a reply: your answer, when he asked something, and under it one line saying the card was updated, with your `changes`. Nothing more goes there, because a whole card under his words buries what he came back for. A version you post on your own, with no reply since the last one, goes at the bottom as a whole card too, with a line in the channel, since the thread may be days old. Either way your `changes` line is how he learns what is different, usually on a phone where he can't lay two cards side by side. Say what moved on the card since the version he saw, what his reply changed first: "Japan dropped, per your reply." The answer to his question goes in `--answer`, never in `changes`: the line sits right under your answer, so an answer in it is said twice, and on a version with no answer it is buried as one more edit. Under "Old catalog. All 10 of his lessons since November 15 came from it.", the line reads "the email now offers to move him to the new lessons", not "he is on the old catalog, so the email now offers...". A restatement of the card or a list of every edit sends him back to comparing versions by eye ("I see an accumulating set of copies ... and can't figure out what is new"). Each version must actually change what a yes covers. A detail that moved without changing what he would say (which of a batch this is, a slot a few minutes off, a cost still inside its range) is fixed in the work and the card stays as it is, as "Qualifiers" in "Writing to Amir" says: every post on a card makes him reread it to learn whether his answer still holds. An answer to his question is not a version. A new version after a yes replaces that yes too, so wait for his answer on the new one. After a decline, a new proposal is a new card, not a version.

## Reviewing cards for the approver

Some cards get a second reader before the approver answers: the CFO reads every money card, so what an ask does to the company's cash sits beside it when he decides. If that's your job:

- **Take the role from your own session:** `approvals review --types budget,purchase,money --agent "<your name>"`. There is one reviewer, so running it from a new session (a restore, a fresh start) moves the role there, with any messages still waiting for it. A pod member's role stays with its tab, whichever session runs there. `approvals review` says who holds it, and `--off` ends it.
- **Each new card of those types, each new version and each of the approver's answers reaches you** as an `[Approvals]` message naming the card; `approvals show AP-0013` reads it. These need no acknowledgement. When you pick work back up, `approvals inbox` hands you what came while you were stopped, as it does for askers.
- **Your read goes in the card's thread:** `approvals note AP-0013 --file read.md`, under your name. Write what he should weigh that the card doesn't show, verdict first, in a few lines of his words: "A one-time $3,000 leaves the pod $2,400 under its month's grant. My read: worth it while a trial stays under $25." A restatement of the card adds nothing. The note changes nothing on the card, and he may answer before it lands, so post it soon and never hold other work for it. A new version gets another note only when it changes your read.
- **The card stays the asker's.** Don't answer, revise or record anything on it; his answers reach you so your own records follow them, a yes booked the same day.

## Answering cards for the approver

The workspace's default approver can set one agent, his deputy, to answer for him the cards he approves every time, so that what reaches him is what needs him. Cards for another approver wait for that person. If that's your job, which cards to approve is taught in your own instructions. This is how the tool carries your call.

- **Take the role from your own session:** `approvals deputy --agent "<your name>"`. There is one deputy, so running it from a new session moves the role there, with what was waiting for it. A pod member's role stays with its tab, whichever session runs there. `approvals deputy` says who holds it and whether auto-approval is on. Turning it on or pausing it is the approver's, with the button under his morning summary; until he turns it on you run as a dry run.
- **Every new card for the default approver reaches you** as an `[Approvals]` message, and so does each new version, each withdrawal, and everything he says or does on his cards other than a plain yes. These need no acknowledgement. When you pick work back up, `approvals inbox` hands you what came while you were stopped.
- You hear when the approver hands a card to a teammate and when one comes back unanswered, and `--also` is refused on a version a teammate already holds.
- **Read the card and the record before you call.** `approvals show AP-0013` is the card as he gets it, and `approvals pictures AP-0013` saves its pictures where you can look at them. `approvals history --agent "<asker>"` and `approvals history --type creative` show how recent cards like it went and what he said on them; `approvals history --days 1` shows what he has said lately on anything.
- **Make one call on each card:** `approvals call AP-0013 --approve --why "<why>"` or `approvals call AP-0013 --his --why "<why>"`. Only `--his` takes `--also <name>`, which sends the card to one teammate who reads the same why as the approver and can hand the card back, in which case you are told. The card waits for that person's working hours unless the call carries `--urgent`. The why is for him, one or two sentences in his words for things: what made this routine, or what on this card needs him. He reads it cold, on the card and in his morning summary of what was approved for him: "The ninth Last Night's Hand Short, made and checked the way the eight you approved were." A why that restates the card, or says only "routine", gives him nothing he could correct.
- **A dry run and the real thing are the same call.** In a dry run the card shows what you would have done, and nothing is approved. With auto-approval on, your approve reaches the asker as a yes, and the card says it was approved for him, by you, with your why. Call every card as if it were on: the mode is his to set, never a reason to call differently.
- **Your approve covers the whole card.** Every open row is approved, rows the asker marked as needing their own yes included. Where the card asks for a pick, at its own level or on a row, your approve takes the pick the card names, and the asker is told to do exactly that option. So approve a card with a pick only when the named pick is the one he would take; otherwise leave it for him and say which way you lean in your why. A card that asks for a pick and names none is refused, because there is nothing for an approval to take.
- **Your call is on the version you read.** A new version needs a call of its own. A version takes one call, with one exception: a card you called without `--also` in a dry run that is still waiting on him can be called again once auto-approval is on, and that call is the one that stands. A card he or his teammate already answered, or that was withdrawn, is refused, because there is nothing left to call.
- **The card stays the asker's, and the yes stays yours.** Don't answer, revise or record anything on a card. An approval you give is recorded as yours, made for him, never as his words, and he can take it back with one tap. When he does, or replies on a card you approved, it reaches you: that is him correcting your reading.
- **Looking back:** `approvals history --calls` lists your calls beside what he then did.

## Other commands

- `approvals answer AP-0013 "<answer>"` posts your answer in the thread, its first sentence in bold, and hands a card that was back with you to him as it stands. On an approved or withdrawn card it posts and changes nothing else: an answer to something he asked after it closed. A fact that moved after his yes is a new version when it changes what the yes covers, and otherwise stays in the work: from November 17 to 24 agents put 325 corrections and additions on cards and under Slack posts beside them, and most changed nothing he would do (November 24: "I just need the pods to stop spamming me with their stream of consciousness"). When you answered him somewhere else, it can carry the link.
- `approvals ack AP-0013 "<text>"` posts one line in the thread: what you understood and what you're doing now after a decision, or what's coming and when if a reply will take a while.
- `approvals inbox --agent "<name>"` hands you the answers waiting for you: ones that came while your session wasn't running, and ones that couldn't be typed in. Each is handed out once.
- `approvals withdraw AP-0013 "<why>"` takes a card down: it shows your reason and loses its buttons.
- `approvals record AP-0013 --approved --words "<their exact words>" --where "<where they said it>"` records a yes or no the approver gave outside Slack, so the card shows it. Add `--part 2` (or the row's id) for one row of a set, or use `--choice "<the option's name>"` for a pick.
- `approvals status AP-0013 "<what happened>"` keeps a posted card true in place (above); `--part-of "<initiative>"` or `--stands-alone` sets what it belongs to.
- `approvals show AP-0013` shows one request. `approvals list --open` shows what's open.
