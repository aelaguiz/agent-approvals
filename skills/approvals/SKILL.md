# Approvals

When an action needs your approver's yes first, put one card in front of them that they can decide from in a few seconds, keep working, and act on their answer exactly when it comes back. A good card gets a fast yes or a useful correction. A vague one gets "what is the question?" or no answer at all, and the work stalls. Everything you post there, the card and every reply under it, is read the same way: once, cold, on a phone, between other work, and taken as fact.

## What matters most

- **Ask only when a yes is actually required.** If your instructions or a standing rule already let you act, act and report what you did. A card that didn't need asking spends the approver's attention and teaches them to skim cards.
- **A card is for a change that is ready to happen, never for deciding to build something on the card alone.** It shows the exact thing that will go out or switch on: the ad, the reply, the price, the message, a flag on code that already exists. Whether to build a feature, a new screen, or an experiment whose variant needs app or server work first is a different decision, which the approver makes from the report or deck that carries its case, size and mocks. Amir, October 19, 2026, declining two cards that asked him to approve building experiments: "features are not built via an approval Slack message. That's an entirely different decision process because I'm never going to just click yes and then let an agent build some feature." So keep that call a proposal in your report, deck or plan, unless the rules of the repository you work in send it to the approver as a card that links that deck; then the card is the deck's front page, with what users would see and what decides it. Once the thing is built, switching it on can be a card.
- **Every card opens with why it's worth doing, and says what it belongs to.** The approver decides on value, and a card that shows only what will happen and how much leaves him guessing what it's for: "I understand the what but there's no why," on a card that laid out a puzzle change in five tidy facts. `why` is the first thing under the title: what the business gets and why now, in a sentence or two of his terms. For a test, it's the question the result answers and what you'd do on each answer, never a gain nobody knows yet. If you can't write it, or don't believe it, the card isn't ready. One agent sent a card to build something its own analysis said wasn't worth doing, because building felt cheap, and a why line didn't save it. `part_of` names the initiative in his words for it, or is `null` when the card stands alone; `approvals status AP-0013 --part-of "<initiative>"` sets it on a card already out. When the work lives on a Basecamp card, name it: `approvals submit card.json --basecamp <card's link>` (again for each card it moves, or `"basecamp"` in the JSON). His "Part of:" then opens that card, and the card keeps a step, written as you, that shows where the approval stands (waiting on him, his answer and its time, and "not yet seen by" you until you acknowledge), checked once you're done. You change nothing for it: your usual `ack`, `answer`, `status` and `done` keep it true. A to-do's link (a Finance to-do) gets only the link. Don't copy card numbers into the Basecamp card's text; the step is the record there.
- **A request to do it is not the approver's yes.** Tasks often come from a teammate, another agent, a brief or a schedule. Unless the approver is plainly the one telling you to do this, treat it as a request that still needs their yes. When they do tell you directly, that is the yes; don't send them a card about their own instruction.
- **The card is how the question reaches them.** Asking in this conversation only works if the approver is the one reading it. When it might not be them, submit a card and say in the conversation that you did.
- **Never take the action before the answer.** Three things count as a yes: the card's approve button, which reaches you as an `[Approvals]` message saying it was approved; the approver's own words naming this request, such as "post it" on the card or a yes given elsewhere that you record with `approvals record`; or his deputy's approval. The deputy is an agent he has set to answer for him the kinds of card he approves every time. Its yes arrives as the same `[Approvals]` message, says it was approved for him and why, and counts as his for exactly what the card describes. Nothing about how you ask changes: the deputy leaves him whatever needs him, and he decides from the card you wrote, so write every card for him. A friendly reply to a long report is not a yes.
- **Put the real thing on the card.** Show the exact reply under the post it answers, the ad itself, the price table with today beside proposed, the email word for word. If the approver would have to open something else to know what they're agreeing to, the card isn't ready.
- **One decision per card, answered where he reads it.** He reads top to bottom on a phone and answers with the buttons under what he just read, so whatever a button decides sits right above it. A row that says "see below", or words behind a Read button, sends him down and back up with the row held in his head; four customer replies as four rows behind four Read buttons was "this weird homework," and he asked for separate cards instead. So rows are for parts of one decision that a line or two decides each, like a release's stores; anything that needs its own reading (a reply to a person, a design, a plan, a spend whose case takes more than a few lines) is its own card, and cards that belong together go out together as a set, each showing "1 of 4". Together is half of it: four related cards arriving one by one over half an hour got "they should come together rather than individually." A card also asks one kind of yes: a card asking to ship what's built that also carries what reads like decisions to build left him asking which it wanted. [references/cards.md](references/cards.md#one-card-or-a-set) has the cases each way; read it before any card with rows or more than one card.
- **Ask only for a yes that someone will carry out.** A yes is an instruction, and the approver stops tracking the request once they give it. If you can't act on it yourself, the card names who will, and you get the yes to them and see it through. Otherwise the yes lands nowhere while the approver believes it's handled.
- **Ask once.** Never post the same card again. If the card must be answered by a time that is near and the approver may not have seen it, the rules of the repository you work in say whether and how to remind them; without such a rule, don't. No answer by the deadline means no.
- **Take a card down as soon as you stop standing behind it.** An open card has a working approve button, and approvers clear several cards in a minute. When you drop the version it shows, the moment passes, or the facts change, withdraw it with `approvals withdraw` and say why, or post the version you do stand behind. On October 19 a card was approved nine hours after its agent had said in the thread that nothing would post from it, because its button still worked.
- **The thread is the approver's whole view of your work.** They can't see your session; 📨 on their reply only means the message was typed into it. So:
  - **Reply once, and make it the answer.** When he asks something, the first thing he should meet is the answer, verdict first, in the words he asked in: "No, it isn't worth doing now," "It doesn't change it: testers keep press-and-hold." A "Got it: you want to know whether..." post that restates his question and promises an answer is one more thing to read before the one he wanted, and a run of acknowledgements, new versions and self-corrections makes him reread all of them: "Please stop sending me the same approvals and just ... talk to me." Your answer or your next version is your acknowledgement. Post a separate `approvals ack` only when the real reply will take more than a few minutes, saying what's coming and when; and when he says yes, one line on what you're doing now is the right first post.
  - **Promise only what you know you can do.** "Starting it now" before checking whether there's room to start it becomes a correction three minutes later. Check first, or say what you're checking.
  - **"Done" means the change is real for the people it's for, and you've seen it:** live for the users his yes named, a scheduled post or puzzle reading back scheduled, an app change in a release users have. Your part finishing, a hand-off or a schedule isn't done; until it is, the card says where it stands and who it waits on. A card marked done while the setting it asked for was still off got "Did this happen?" back.
  - **If you end up doing something different, say so in the thread.**
- **Act only on answers that reach you:** an `[Approvals]` message in this session, or one `approvals inbox` hands you. A request another session made belongs to that session even when it's for the same change, including an earlier run of your scheduled job. A session you continue is different: if this session opened with a summary of earlier work, that work is yours, its open cards included. If the change you were asked for is already requested and still open, don't post a duplicate; say which request it's waiting on.
- **Don't wait around, and look for your answers whenever you pick work back up.** After submitting, keep working on something else, or end your turn saying which request you're waiting on. If the approver stops this session, an answer that comes meanwhile waits for you, and nothing types it in when you're back. So whenever you pick work back up with cards out (a resumed or restored session, a new run of a scheduled job), run `approvals inbox --agent "<name>"` before anything else and act on what it hands you.

## How the approver reads, and how to write for it

He knows his product, his systems and his field better than you do, and he holds none of your session: not your plan, your labels, or what you said an hour ago. Writing fails him two opposite ways, and he names both: too much, and he hunts for the point (he asked for "less words in a more direct way that takes less processing to understand"); too compressed, and he has to decode it ("you keep answering me cryptically"). The fix for both is fewer things, each said whole, in his words. Before anything goes up, read it as him, cold, on a phone, and fix the first phrase he wouldn't say himself, the first number he couldn't place, the first sentence he'd have to ask about. Where your machine has `$readable-reports`, give the words its pass. No em dashes.

- **His words, not yours.** Name each thing the way he does, or by what it does for users. Your working labels read literally to him: a card that called a new AI "production's recipe," because it was trained with production's settings, got "You're saying switch production to production." A card that described the mechanism, "Extend sign-ins made before October 8 to the 1,000 days we now set," got "Is this ... English?"; the same change as "Stop signing out users who signed in before October 8" got a yes. A card, issue or pull request number goes after the plain name as a link, never in its place and never as the explanation: "Don't explain things to me by referencing a ... code thing." His field's terms need no help; your process's terms always do.
- **What it is and where it stands, before any detail.** Say the whole change before its edge cases: a card that opened on a locked-puzzle case got "Is this literally just for puzzles or what?" when the change covered every purchase. Give its state in a word he'd use: built, live for half of users, waiting on Apple. If it changes or reverses something he approved, say that first and name what. A test running beside another is called a side test, so it doesn't read as more of the main one.
- **Fewer things, each said whole.** The card draws each fact as its own block with its label above it, so a short label and a sentence or two each; a value that is a list puts each item on a line starting with "- ", and they show as bullets. Aim for about 100 to 180 words of your own. What he's approving (a message's exact words, an ad's words, a release's changes) doesn't count against that and is never cut to fit, and a round of ads counts per ad. What proves your work (the checks, the research, the numbers behind the numbers) goes in `evidence`, posted in the thread.
- **Every number with its comparison:** what it counts, over which days, against what. "$700, up from $450"; a rate where a rate exists; money for the US and outside it apart.
- **Leave out how your systems work; keep everything his yes decides.** He built the systems, so don't walk him through flags, server fields, build and deploy steps, or how you'd switch it off. But who a change reaches, how many and for how long, what it spends, which release or store and when, what it takes away, what can't be undone, and the new words users will read are his decision, and they stay. A cost to your team is noise; a cost that moves his date or his money is part of the choice. When it touches sign-in, payments or something users already have, say so in one plain line.
- **Only what's true.** He acts on "checked", "done", "live" and "waiting on you" without checking, and one false line makes him audit every card after it: a card said its script had been through cold readers and a final read, and he heard the lines nobody had read ("did anybody read this script?"). "Checked" names checks that ran on exactly what he's looking at. Anything "you said" is quoted from the record with its date, or left out. When a card carries numbers or claims, have a fresh reader check them against their sources before it goes up; a cold check has caught a seventh of a card's claims wrong.
- **Only what's his, once.** Never his own instruction sent back for approval ("I ... told you to send the email"), a call that's yours to make, an engineering question inside a product decision, or a phrase for him to reply with. When a standing rule is what makes it his (every new ad, taking a screen away), say which in a line. Related asks ready at the same time arrive together, as rows or a set; related asks that ripen hours apart don't wait for each other; unrelated asks ready in the same minute are separate cards, not a set.

## Asking

1. **Write the card as JSON.** Before writing, read [references/cards.md](references/cards.md) for the type and the kind you need: what the approver judges it by, what it must include, how to shape it around how he'll judge it (today beside after, a row each, a pick, a batch, his own words to edit), and a full example.
2. **Submit it** from your own session, on the machine you run on: `approvals submit card.json --agent "<your name as the approver knows you>"`. A set is several files in one command, `approvals submit a.json b.json c.json`: each is its own card with its own answer, posted one after another and marked "1 of 3"; if any is refused, none is posted. The command learns from your session where to type the answer, so a card posted from another shell, such as over ssh, could never be answered to you, and the command refuses it. It prints the request number (for example AP-0013) and a link, one of each per card. If it refuses the card, it says why; fix that and submit again.
3. **Carry on.** Do other work, or end your turn.

A `budget`, `purchase` or `money` card also reaches the reviewer for money when one is set up (the CFO), and its read of your card lands in the thread, often before the approver answers; so don't ask it about the card separately, and remember its note isn't an answer: yours comes only from the approver.

Every card needs:
- `type`, one of: `creative`, `reply`, `budget`, `purchase`, `money`, `player_message`, `price`, `experiment`, `page`, `content`, `email`, `account`;
- `title`, the action said plainly ("Move $20,000 from savings to operating", not "Transfer approval");
- `why`, one or two sentences on what the business gets and why now;
- `part_of`, the initiative it belongs to, or `null`;
- `basecamp`, optional: the link of the Basecamp card (or to-do) it serves, or a list of them;
- `facts`, a list of `[label, value]` pairs;
- `approves` and `does_not_approve`, which mark the edges of the yes.

Some types also need `quotes`, a `table`, or an `image` or `video`; the cards reference says which. Pictures must read when he opens them full size and zooms in: a screen at the phone's own resolution, a slide exported at two or three times its size, cut to what the card is about.

**Evidence has to open in Slack.** A path to a file on your machine posted as text opens nothing, so give the file's path (alone or after a label, like `"Recipe: /path/recipe.json"`) and it's attached to the thread, or link a page the approver can open: a doc, a sheet, a share link.

## When the answer arrives

Answers arrive as messages starting with `[Approvals]` that quote the approver's words exactly: typed into this session while it's running, or printed by `approvals inbox` for one that came while it wasn't. For a yes, a no, a pick or a set of items, first post one line with `approvals ack AP-0013 "<what you understood and what you're doing now>"`, such as "Got it: posting the reply now, then I'll mark it done." For a reply or a question, your answer or your next version is the acknowledgement; post nothing before it. A deadline passing needs no acknowledgement.

| The message says | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| It was approved | Do exactly what the card described, nothing more. Then run `approvals done AP-0013 "<what you did and how you checked it>"`. |
| It was approved for him by his deputy | The same as his own yes: do exactly what the card described, nothing more, then `approvals done`. The message carries the deputy's reason, which changes nothing about what the card covers. It covers every row, the ones you marked as needing their own yes included. If your card asked for a pick, the message names the pick it took, which is the one your card named: do exactly what the card says for that option, nothing else. |
| He took back an approval his deputy gave | It is not approved any more. If you haven't acted, don't. If you have, undo what can be undone, and say on the card where it stands with `approvals status`. Your acknowledgement says which of the two it is; he may say more in the thread. |
| It was declined | Drop this proposal; never send it again or a lightly changed copy. A no is to the proposal, not necessarily to what it was for: usually the goal still stands and the next proposal is a new card that's actually different; sometimes the goal is dead too. Your acknowledgement says which you took it to be. |
| He removed it from his list | Nothing on it is approved, and it's no verdict on the idea: he's saying the card shouldn't be waiting on him. Drop it and don't post it again. If what it was for still matters, work out why it didn't belong in front of him (yours to decide, already settled, out of date) before asking again. |
| A question in the thread | Answer it: `approvals answer AP-0013 "<the verdict, then the facts behind it>"`. That is your acknowledgement, and it hands the card back to him as it stands. Post a new version only if what a yes covers changes. |
| A reply asking for a change | Post the next version: `approvals revise AP-0013 card.json`, with a `changes` field saying what moved. If you truly can't continue without one fact, ask instead: `approvals ask AP-0013 "<one question>"`. If the reply plainly approves the card as it stands ("post it"), record that with `approvals record AP-0013 --approved --words "<their words>" --where "Slack thread"` and act on it; if it plainly declines, record it with `--declined` and drop it. |
| An answer to your question | If it asks for a change, carry on with `approvals revise`. If it lets the card go ahead as it stands, even one he had already approved ("sure, schedule it whenever" after you asked which hour to use once the first one passed), record it with `approvals record AP-0013 --approved --words "<their words>" --where "Slack thread"` (on a card with rows, add `--part <row>` when the answer was about one row), act on it, then run `approvals done`. |
| An answer on one part of a set (`part 2 of 4`) | It answers that part only. Act on that part as above; the message says where the other parts stand. Parts you leave exactly as they are keep their answers in the next version. |
| He picked an option | Do exactly what the card said for that option, nothing else. |
| He approved some items | Do only the items the message lists; the ones it leaves out are a no. |
| He approved with his own edited words | Use his words exactly, as the message quotes them (a JSON string, line breaks and all), not your draft. |
| He answered your offer to stop asking | "Stop asking for these": record it as a standing exception in your own rules, with the date and exactly what it covers, and do that kind without a card from now on. "Keep asking": keep sending cards. |
| No answer by the deadline | It's not approved, unless the card's `if_no_answer` said what happens then: do that. Don't resubmit it. On a set, only the parts still open close; answered parts stand. |

**After the yes, keep the card true until the thing is really out.** He stops tracking a request once he answers it, and the card is where he looks to see whether it happened. So post each step on the card as it happens: `approvals status AP-0013 "Built, and live for half of new users"`, then `approvals status AP-0013 "First read at about $10 spent" --next` for what comes next, `--part 2` (or the row's id) for one row of a set, and `--headline "Released: three lesson fixes"` once it's out. When it waits on someone other than him (a teammate's design note, Apple's review, the next money ask), say who and by when with `--waiting-on`: the card shows it and stays off his morning list until your next line. `--on-you` is only for a condition he alone can clear ("approved, but I'll play it myself before it merges"); a card that says it waits on him when nothing does gets "There's nothing for me to do." On a message sent as him, `--their-answer` puts the other side's reply on the card. Each line is one short sentence he can read at a glance. These redraw the card in place and notify no one; `done` still marks the end, when it's real.

**A new version replaces the old one.** The approver sees only the new version, at the bottom of the thread and in the first post, with a line in the channel pointing to it; the old one shrinks to a line he can open if he wants it. So your `changes` line, which opens the new card above his own words, is how he learns what is different, usually on a phone where he can't lay two cards side by side. Say what moved since the version he saw, the answer to his reply first: "Japan dropped, per your reply." A restatement of the card or a list of every edit sends him back to comparing versions by eye ("I see an accumulating set of copies ... and can't figure out what is new"). Each version must actually change what a yes covers; an answer to his question is not a version. A new version after a yes replaces that yes too, so wait for his answer on the new one. After a decline, a new proposal is a new card, not a version.

## Reviewing cards for the approver

Some cards get a second reader before the approver answers: the CFO reads every money card, so what an ask does to the company's cash sits beside it when he decides. If that's your job:

- **Take the role from your own session:** `approvals review --types budget,purchase,money --agent "<your name>"`. There is one reviewer, so running it from a new session (a restore, a fresh start) moves the role there, with any messages still waiting for it. `approvals review` says who holds it, and `--off` ends it.
- **Each new card of those types, each new version and each of the approver's answers reaches you** as an `[Approvals]` message naming the card; `approvals show AP-0013` reads it. These need no acknowledgement. When you pick work back up, `approvals inbox` hands you what came while you were stopped, as it does for askers.
- **Your read goes in the card's thread:** `approvals note AP-0013 --file read.md`, under your name. Write what he should weigh that the card doesn't show, verdict first, in a few lines of his words: "A one-time $3,000 leaves the pod $2,400 under its month's grant. My read: worth it while a trial stays under $25." A restatement of the card adds nothing. The note changes nothing on the card, and he may answer before it lands, so post it soon and never hold other work for it. A new version gets another note only when it changes your read.
- **The card stays the asker's.** Don't answer, revise or record anything on it; his answers reach you so your own records follow them, a yes booked the same day.

## Answering cards for the approver

The approver can set one agent, his deputy, to answer for him the cards he approves every time, so that what reaches him is what needs him. If that's your job, which cards to approve is taught in your own instructions. This is how the tool carries your call.

- **Take the role from your own session:** `approvals deputy --agent "<your name>"`. There is one deputy, so running it from a new session moves the role there, with what was waiting for it. `approvals deputy` says who holds it and whether auto-approval is on. Turning it on or pausing it is the approver's, with the button under his morning summary; until he turns it on you run as a dry run.
- **Every new card reaches you** as an `[Approvals]` message, and so does each new version, each withdrawal, and everything he says or does on any card other than a plain yes. These need no acknowledgement. When you pick work back up, `approvals inbox` hands you what came while you were stopped.
- **Read the card and the record before you call.** `approvals show AP-0013` is the card as he gets it, and `approvals pictures AP-0013` saves its pictures where you can look at them. `approvals history --agent "<asker>"` and `approvals history --type creative` show how recent cards like it went and what he said on them; `approvals history --days 1` shows what he has said lately on anything.
- **Make one call on each card:** `approvals call AP-0013 --approve --why "<why>"` or `approvals call AP-0013 --his --why "<why>"`. The why is for him, one or two sentences in his words for things: what made this routine, or what on this card needs him. He reads it cold, on the card and in his morning summary of what was approved for him: "The ninth Last Night's Hand Short, made and checked the way the eight you approved were." A why that restates the card, or says only "routine", gives him nothing he could correct.
- **A dry run and the real thing are the same call.** In a dry run the card shows what you would have done, and nothing is approved. With auto-approval on, your approve reaches the asker as a yes, and the card says it was approved for him, by you, with your why. Call every card as if it were on: the mode is his to set, never a reason to call differently.
- **Your approve covers the whole card.** Every open row is approved, rows the asker marked as needing their own yes included. Where the card asks for a pick, at its own level or on a row, your approve takes the pick the card names, and the asker is told to do exactly that option. So approve a card with a pick only when the named pick is the one he would take; otherwise leave it for him and say which way you lean in your why. A card that asks for a pick and names none is refused, because there is nothing for an approval to take.
- **Your call is on the version you read.** A new version needs a call of its own. A card he has already answered, or that was withdrawn, is refused, because there is nothing left to call.
- **The card stays the asker's, and the yes stays yours.** Don't answer, revise or record anything on a card. An approval you give is recorded as yours, made for him, never as his words, and he can take it back with one tap. When he does, or replies on a card you approved, it reaches you: that is him correcting your reading.
- **Looking back:** `approvals history --calls` lists your calls beside what he then did.

## Other commands

- `approvals answer AP-0013 "<answer>"` posts your answer in the thread, its first sentence in bold, and hands a card that was back with you to him as it stands. On an approved or withdrawn card it posts and changes nothing else: a correction after his yes ("your yes stands unless you say otherwise"), or an answer to something he asked after it closed. When you answered him somewhere else, it can carry the link.
- `approvals ack AP-0013 "<text>"` posts one line in the thread: what you understood and what you're doing now after a decision, or what's coming and when if a reply will take a while.
- `approvals inbox --agent "<name>"` hands you the answers waiting for you: ones that came while your session wasn't running, and ones that couldn't be typed in. Each is handed out once.
- `approvals withdraw AP-0013 "<why>"` takes a card down: it shows your reason and loses its buttons.
- `approvals record AP-0013 --approved --words "<their exact words>" --where "<where they said it>"` records a yes or no the approver gave outside Slack, so the card shows it. Add `--part 2` (or the row's id) for one row of a set, or use `--choice B` for a pick.
- `approvals status AP-0013 "<what happened>"` keeps a posted card true in place (above); `--part-of "<initiative>"` or `--stands-alone` sets what it belongs to.
- `approvals show AP-0013` shows one request. `approvals list --open` shows what's open.
